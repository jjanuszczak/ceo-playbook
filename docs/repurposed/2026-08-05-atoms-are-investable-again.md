## LinkedIn Candidates

### LinkedIn Option 1

Capital-intensive businesses are investable again, but not because investors forgot the pain of Cleantech 1.0.

The standard has changed.

Capex is acceptable when it is tied to:

- Visible demand
- A repeatable deployment model
- Regulatory progress
- Clear unit economics
- A physical bottleneck that customers need solved

That is why Base Power and Valar Atomics matter.

Base is not just selling home batteries. It is turning distributed storage into a grid asset.

Valar is not just pitching nuclear. It has a DOE-recognized reactor milestone.

So what? The market is not choosing atoms over software. It is choosing real constraints over easy stories.

### LinkedIn Option 2

The twist in the "atoms are back" story is that enterprise software still has a strong hand.

Weak AI wrappers are vulnerable. Generic tools with unclear margins deserve pressure.

But enterprise SaaS with real workflow depth is different.

The durable businesses own:

- Systems of record
- Embedded integrations
- Security and compliance trust
- Procurement credibility
- Proprietary data
- Measurable customer ROI

AI makes software easier to build. It does not make enterprise replacement easy.

So what? Do not underwrite software by asking whether AI can copy the feature. Ask whether AI can replace the workflow, risk controls, and trust layer.

### LinkedIn Option 3

The most useful investment question for the AI era may be simple:

What is the constraint?

If the constraint is power, storage, manufacturing, or permitting, then atoms become investable again.

If the constraint is workflow ownership, compliance, distribution, or trusted data, then enterprise software still compounds.

If the constraint is just "we added AI to the interface," the story is probably weak.

Base Power and Valar Atomics show why capex can attract serious capital again. The SaaS rebound shows why software is not finished.

So what? Stop debating atoms versus software. Find the scarce layer and ask who controls it.

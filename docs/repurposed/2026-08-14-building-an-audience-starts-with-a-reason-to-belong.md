## LinkedIn Candidates

### LinkedIn Option 1

Audience-building does not start with a bigger mailing list.

It starts with a credible reason to belong.

Last year, I helped run a professional speaking series for expatriates in the Philippines. It started with fewer than 20 people and grew to more than 110 at one of the latest events.

The mechanics were simple, but not easy:

- A legitimate mission
- A defined audience
- Member-led invitations
- Manual follow-up
- Specific programming
- Realistic assumptions about no-shows

The strongest insight: the room matters, but the contact record matters more.

So what? Do not build an audience around promotion. Build it around a repeated moment where the right people think, "this was made for me."

### LinkedIn Option 2

One of the most useful event lessons I learned was also the most uncomfortable:

Registration is not attendance.

For virtual events, roughly half of registrants did not attend live. ON24's 2025 benchmark data reported a 57% registration-to-attendance conversion rate. For our face-to-face events, even with a nominal fee, we still saw roughly 30 to 35% no-shows.

That changed how I planned the economics.

Registration is demand.
Attendance is capacity planning.

They are not the same number.

So what? Build the budget around expected attendance, not the optimistic registration count. Sponsors can reduce risk later, but they should not be the initial assumption.

### LinkedIn Option 3

The urge to automate too early is strong.

We resisted it.

In the early days of our professional speaking series, we made the materials ourselves, posted from personal accounts, sent careful mail-merge emails, and called the people who really needed to be in the room.

It was manual.
It was inefficient.
It was the work.

Manual operations showed us which messages worked, who actually attended, which topics traveled, and where the registration process broke.

So what? Automation scales what you already understand. If you automate too early, you scale assumptions and lose the feedback that would have fixed them.

### LinkedIn Option 4

The best speaker is not always the biggest name.

For a professional event series serving expatriates in the Philippines, one of our strongest virtual sessions came from a young academic and historian discussing expatriate contract workers in Philippine history.

He was not a celebrity.
That was not a weakness.

The topic was specific, surprising, and directly connected to the audience's lived experience.

Many event organizers borrow status, then hope the audience will care. The better move is to start with the group's real interests, then find the person who can satisfy them.

So what? Book the speaker your audience will talk about the next day.

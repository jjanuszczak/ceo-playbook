Some valuations look insane because we keep using the wrong mental model.

We look at an AI company with thin current revenue, heavy compute spend, and an asset base that barely appears on a balance sheet. Then we ask an old question: what multiple of today's cash flow or revenue could justify this price?

Sometimes the answer is simple. Nothing justifies it. Plenty of AI companies are wrappers, resellers, or workflow demos with no durable learning asset underneath.

But that is not the whole story.

{{< quick-answer >}}
Learning Capital is financial capital converted into intelligence assets that improve through use: models, proprietary data, agents, feedback loops, evaluation systems, and organizational cognition. It helps explain why some AI-era valuations stretch traditional DCF and revenue multiples, because markets are trying to price ownership of a compounding learning process rather than only current cash flow.
{{< /quick-answer >}}

The better investor question is not "why is this company worth so much today?"

It is: **what is the company learning that competitors cannot cheaply copy, and how fast does that learning compound?**

## What is Learning Capital?

Learning Capital is financial capital converted into self-improving intelligence assets whose value grows by being used.

That includes trained models, proprietary datasets, evaluation libraries, human feedback systems, agent workflows, domain-specific memory, and the organizational routines that make all of those assets useful. A model by itself is not enough. A dataset by itself is not enough. The asset is the loop: deploy, observe, learn, improve, redeploy.

Economists Hans Gersbach, Evgenij Komarov, and Richard von Maydell model this formally as [AI as self-learning capital](https://cepr.org/publications/dp17221). Their core point is direct: AI productivity can rise through use and through training with data. In the later [Economic Modelling paper](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/bitstreams/578914d1-1ce7-45ca-9dda-cfb7dca1a5c9/download), they describe AI capital as improving through application and interacting with applied research through spillovers.

That is a different production function from a plant, a truck fleet, or a normal SaaS codebase.

Traditional capital depreciates. You buy the machine. It produces output. Maintenance keeps it alive, but time works against it.

Learning Capital can behave differently. Every customer interaction, failed task, edge case, correction, simulation, and workflow trace can make the system more valuable. Use is not only consumption. Use becomes training material.

## Why do DCF and revenue multiples misread the asset?

DCF is not wrong. Multiples are not useless.

They are incomplete when the most important asset is being created off balance sheet and improves through deployment.

An AI company may spend heavily today on compute, engineering, data operations, and customer implementation. A standard accounting view may treat much of that as expense. But strategically, some of that spend is building a learning asset: a dataset competitors do not have, an evaluation harness that compresses future model risk, a workflow agent that gets better with every completed task, or a customer-specific operating memory that raises switching cost.

Ocean Tomo's [intangible asset study](https://oceantomo.com/intangible-asset-market-value-study/) is useful background here. Its 2025 release says intangible assets represent roughly 92% of S&P 500 market capitalization, up from 17% in 1975. The exact percentage is less important than the direction: public markets have already moved from physical assets toward intellectual, relational, data, and software-based assets.

AI pushes that shift one level further.

Intangibles used to be mostly static: brand, patents, customer relationships, software, process knowledge. Learning Capital is dynamic. It can appreciate because the asset participates in its own improvement.

That is why the valuation frame has to change. The market is not only buying current ARR. It is buying a claim on a learning loop.

This connects directly to [the question of whether more venture funding makes startups worse]({{< ref "articles/does-more-venture-funding-make-startups-worse" >}}). Capital is useful when it improves the learning rate. It is dangerous when it only makes an unproven motion more expensive.

## What should investors actually underwrite?

Start with learning rate.

Not a vague claim that "the model gets smarter." Underwrite whether each dollar of capital creates a measurable increase in intelligence, reliability, autonomy, or economic throughput.

The diligence frame should include:

1. **Data exclusivity.** Does the company see data competitors cannot access, or is it recycling public inputs?
2. **Feedback quality.** Does use produce clean correction signals, or only noisy activity?
3. **Capability compounding.** Does each deployment make the next task cheaper, safer, faster, or more autonomous?
4. **Task horizon.** Can the system handle longer economically relevant workflows over time?
5. **Transferability.** Does learning from one customer, region, or domain improve performance elsewhere?
6. **Moat conversion.** Does learning turn into switching cost, pricing power, lower cost to serve, or better product velocity?
7. **Governance.** Can the company use data legally, securely, and with customer trust?

METR's work on [task-completion time horizons](https://metr.org/time-horizons/) gives investors one useful measurement pattern. It tracks the length of tasks frontier AI systems can complete at defined success rates and reports an exponential trend across recent model generations. The specific curve will change. The investor lesson is more durable: capability is not only about benchmark accuracy. It is about how long a system can operate reliably before human rescue is needed.

That matters because economics sits in the handoff.

A model that handles a two-minute task is a feature. A system that handles a two-hour workflow with traceability, recovery, and auditability starts to look like capital.

## Where is the line between a learning loop and a wrapper?

Most AI businesses will not become Learning Capital companies.

A token reseller with a thin interface is not learning capital. A generic chatbot pointed at a customer's documents is not learning capital. A services firm that manually patches AI output behind the curtain is not learning capital.

The test is whether the system accumulates proprietary, reusable intelligence as it operates.

Take two companies selling into the same finance function.

The first gives analysts a prettier interface to a general model. It saves time, but every customer gets roughly the same underlying capability. If a cheaper model appears tomorrow, the product has a problem.

The second ingests years of workflow decisions, reconciles analyst corrections, builds internal evaluation sets, learns the firm's approval patterns, maps exceptions to outcomes, and turns that memory into faster, safer future work. That company is not only selling software. It is building a knowledge-bearing asset.

This is why corporate data has suddenly become strategic. In [$10 Million Inbox]({{< ref "articles/10-million-inbox" >}}), I wrote about how enterprise email, chat, code, and operating records can become raw material for future intelligence. The same idea applies inside AI companies: the learning record can become the asset.

The wrapper sells access.

The learning loop compounds capability.

## Why does this change venture capital?

Venture capital has always paid for uncertain compounding.

The change is what compounds.

In the last software cycle, investors often underwrote distribution, network effects, gross margin, and category creation. Those still matter. But in AI, a new question sits beside them: does use make the product materially better in a way the next entrant cannot simply rent from the same frontier model provider?

That is why acqui-hires, licensing deals, research teams, and proprietary datasets are being priced so aggressively. Sometimes the buyer is not only buying product or revenue. It is buying a concentrated unit of judgment, trust, model intuition, and learning velocity. I made that point from the people side in [Acqui-Hiring Is Now a Leadership Strategy]({{< ref "articles/acqui-hiring-as-a-people-strategy" >}}). Talent density is part of the learning asset.

The best VC questions become more operational:

- What does the system know after 1,000 uses that it did not know after 100?
- Who owns the feedback?
- How does the company prevent data leakage from becoming trust leakage?
- Does customer implementation deepen the moat or just create services drag?
- Can the learning loop survive model commoditization?
- Is the company building an asset, or only arbitraging a model release cycle?

That last question is brutal. It should be.

## What does the asset base look like now?

The balance sheet is becoming a weaker map of the company.

The real asset base may include:

- Curated proprietary datasets.
- Task traces and correction history.
- Evaluation suites and failure libraries.
- Domain-specific agents.
- Retrieval systems and organizational memory.
- Customer workflow integrations.
- Prompt, policy, and guardrail systems.
- Human expert feedback loops.
- Teams that know how to turn messy operating reality into model improvement.

Some of this will never sit neatly on the balance sheet. That does not make it unreal.

It means investors have to do harder diligence.

They need to ask how intelligence is produced, where it accumulates, who controls it, how it is protected, and whether it changes the economics of the business. The diligence should feel less like a SaaS ARR review and more like inspecting a refinery: inputs, process, yield, waste, safety controls, and output quality.

## What is the allocator checklist?

For allocators, the practical close is simple.

Do not buy "AI exposure." Buy learning economics.

Before underwriting an AI company, fund, or platform, ask:

1. What proprietary learning happens with every unit of use?
2. How is that learning measured?
3. Who owns the data and feedback rights?
4. Does learning reduce cost to serve or increase willingness to pay?
5. Does the moat get stronger as customers use the product?
6. What breaks if the underlying model provider cuts price by 80%?
7. Is the company building autonomy, or only interface convenience?

This is not a singularity argument. It is not a trading signal. It is not a guarantee that every expensive AI company deserves its valuation.

Many will not.

But the right companies are not merely spending capital. They are converting capital into intelligence assets that can learn, compound, and reshape the production function around them.

That is the category investors need to name.

Learning Capital.

The scarce resource is no longer just money, compute, or labor. It is the ability to turn money into intelligence that improves faster than the money is consumed.

Everyone else is still buying yesterday's capital: the kind that depreciates.

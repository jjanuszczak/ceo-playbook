Every generation of bankers thinks it invented data sharing.

The names change. In the 1840s it was commercial credit reporting. In the 1970s it was consumer reporting reform. In the 2010s it was open banking. In the Philippines today, it is open finance, digital lending, alternative data, and real-time credit exchange.

The underlying problem has barely moved: **credit markets break when the lender cannot see the borrower clearly, and the borrower cannot port their own reputation into the next transaction.**

{{< quick-answer >}}
Credit bureaus were the analog predecessor to open banking. They pooled borrower information to reduce information asymmetry, price credit more accurately, and make lending possible beyond personal relationships. Modern open finance extends the same logic through APIs, consent, real-time data, and stronger consumer control.
{{< /quick-answer >}}

This is why the history matters. Open banking is often sold as a software trend, but it is better understood as the next version of credit infrastructure. The lesson is not that every data-sharing scheme is good. The lesson is sharper: data sharing expands access only when trust, accuracy, permission, and recourse are built into the operating model.

That is the real executive question.

## Why did credit bureaus emerge in the first place?

Credit bureaus were born from a very practical problem: distant credit.

After the Panic of 1837, American merchants were extending trade credit across a larger, messier national economy. Personal reputation did not travel well. A wholesaler in New York could not easily know whether a merchant in Ohio, Georgia, or Illinois paid on time, overstated inventory, or had already burned local suppliers.

Lewis Tappan's Mercantile Agency, formed in New York in 1841, solved that problem with a correspondent network. Local contacts gathered information on businesses, and the agency centralized those reports for subscribers. The Library of Congress traces Dun & Bradstreet's roots to that agency, later R.G. Dun & Company, and to John M. Bradstreet's rival ratings business in Cincinnati.

The technology was primitive. The business logic was not.

The Mercantile Agency did three things that still define financial data infrastructure:

- It converted local reputation into portable information.
- It gave lenders a shared view of repayment risk.
- It made credit decisions less dependent on personal relationships alone.

That is open banking without the API.

Consumer credit reporting came later. Equifax traces its origin to the Retail Credit Company, founded in Atlanta in 1899 by Cator and Guy Woolford. That industry eventually helped support mass-market consumer credit, installment finance, auto loans, mortgages, credit cards, and insurance underwriting.

But it also created real abuse. Early consumer reporting mixed repayment facts with character judgments, rumors, and opaque investigative files. The Fair Credit Reporting Act, passed in 1970 and enforced by the FTC and later also shaped by CFPB rulemaking authority, was a response to a simple market failure: if private data infrastructure determines access to credit, consumers need rights to accuracy, access, permissible use, and dispute.

That trade-off has never gone away.

## What did credit bureaus get right?

Credit bureaus worked because they attacked the central defect in credit markets: information asymmetry.

When lenders know less than borrowers, they either decline more applications, charge everyone a higher risk premium, demand collateral, or rely on social proximity. That penalizes exactly the people financial inclusion efforts are supposed to help: new borrowers, migrant workers, small merchants, gig workers, and thin-file consumers.

The World Bank describes credit reporting systems as part of credit infrastructure: institutions, rules, procedures, standards, and technologies that enable the exchange of credit and relevant information. Done well, those systems support responsible access to finance and financial stability.

That is the part many open banking debates underplay.

Credit data sharing is not only a consumer convenience feature. It is market plumbing. It can lower search costs, reduce fraud, improve risk pricing, and make a borrower legible to more than one institution.

For Southeast Asia, this matters. Many borrowers have economic activity but limited formal credit history. A sari-sari store owner, online seller, ride-hailing driver, or small contractor may generate enough cash flow to repay, but not enough conventional bureau history to pass a bank's standard model.

This is where [The Future of Finance]({{< ref "articles/future-of-finance" >}}) still holds up: open finance is powerful when financial services move closer to the underlying economic activity. Payments, lending, identity, and transaction data become part of the same operating fabric.

## Where did credit bureaus go wrong?

The bureau model also teaches the cautionary lesson.

Centralization creates scale, but it also concentrates power. If a report is wrong, the error can follow a consumer across lenders. If data is stale, the borrower gets judged by yesterday's behavior. If the database is breached, the harm is systemic. If the model absorbs biased inputs, the output can look objective while still reproducing exclusion.

The 1970 FCRA was not an anti-innovation law. It was trust infrastructure. It set rules for permissible purpose, accuracy, dispute rights, adverse action notices, and limits on obsolete information.

That is the same pattern I see in modern fintech regulation. The best regulation does not merely slow the market down. It forces the trust layer to catch up with the business model. The same tension shows up in [The Cost of Closed Doors]({{< ref "articles/ikbr-ban" >}}): consumer protection works only when it protects people without trapping them outside better financial infrastructure.

Open banking has to learn this directly. More data does not automatically mean more inclusion. It can also mean more surveillance, more hidden risk selection, and more data leakage. The difference is governance.

Executives should ask five blunt questions before treating any open finance initiative as inclusion:

1. Can the customer understand and revoke the permission?
2. Is the data accurate enough to support a credit decision?
3. Is there a correction path when the data is wrong?
4. Does the lender use the data to expand access, or only to reject faster?
5. Does the system reduce cost of credit, or simply create another rent-taking data layer?

If those answers are weak, the inclusion story is weak.

## How is open banking different from the bureau model?

Credit bureaus and open banking share the same parent problem, but they are not the same design.

The bureau model is usually retrospective. It tells lenders how a borrower behaved on prior credit obligations: tradelines, delinquencies, inquiries, balances, public records, and repayment history.

Open banking and open finance can be more current. They can expose balances, income flows, payment behavior, recurring obligations, merchant revenue, wallet activity, and transaction patterns. The CFPB's personal financial data rights materials frame this as consumer-authorized access to covered financial data. In the Philippines, BSP's Open Finance PH pilot is explicitly built around API technology and customer-responsive financial products.

That changes the underwriting lens.

A traditional bureau asks: did this person repay credit before?

Open finance can ask: does this person have the cash-flow capacity to repay now?

That is a meaningful shift for emerging markets. Many good borrowers do not have deep formal credit files, but they do have transaction histories, repayment traces, device-linked identity signals, merchant cash flow, salary flows, remittance behavior, or utility-like recurring payments.

The opportunity is not to replace bureaus. It is to combine history with live economic behavior.

This is also why [AI-Enabled Bank Infrastructure Matters]({{< ref "articles/ai-enabling-bank-infrastructure-matters" >}}). Better models are useful only when the bank or lender has a clean data layer, permission architecture, and risk workflow underneath them. AI cannot fix a broken trust pipe.

## Why does LenderLink matter in the Philippines?

LenderLink is a useful modern example because it sits between the old bureau logic and the open finance future.

The company describes itself as a credit data aggregator that combines information from digital lenders, collection agencies, and existing credit bureaus. Its platform uses a secure, consent-driven API network, with daily data refreshes, contributor and consumer participation modes, and access to more than 45 million records. Its FAQ positions it as the Philippines' first digital lender credit data exchange, focused on verified borrower records, fraud reduction, and underserved credit access.

That is not open banking in the narrow PSD2 sense of bank-account access. It is closer to open credit infrastructure.

And that may be exactly what the market needs.

The Philippines has a structural credit visibility problem. Formal banking penetration, fragmented lending channels, rapid digital-loan growth, and inconsistent data coverage all create the same defect: lenders see only part of the borrower. That leads to duplicate borrowing, over-indebtedness, blunt rejection rules, and expensive credit.

LenderLink's proposition is that digital lenders and related ecosystems can contribute borrower and repayment data, then receive better real-time visibility in return. That is the original bureau bargain, rebuilt for APIs and a mobile-first credit market.

The strategic angle is important. If LenderLink, or any similar network, helps lenders identify good thin-file borrowers and avoid customers already showing distress elsewhere, it can expand access and reduce defaults at the same time. That is the only inclusion story that survives scrutiny: **lower risk for the lender, better-priced access for the borrower.**

The risk is also clear. Shared credit data must not become a private blacklist with better UX. It needs data protection, contributor discipline, customer consent, auditability, and a practical correction path. The Philippines already has the policy direction through BSP's Open Finance Framework and data privacy law. The hard part is operational execution.

## What should executives take from 180 years of credit data sharing?

The credit bureau story gives financial services leaders a better way to evaluate open banking.

Do not ask whether open banking is new. It is not.

Ask whether this generation has fixed the defects of the last one.

The strongest open finance systems will have four traits:

- **Permission:** Customers know what data moves, why it moves, and how to stop it.
- **Accuracy:** Data is fresh, traceable, and corrected quickly when wrong.
- **Reciprocity:** Contributors improve the network and receive value without creating a closed cartel.
- **Inclusion economics:** Better data lowers loss rates enough to support broader access and fairer pricing.

Credit bureaus proved that shared financial data can unlock credit at scale. They also proved that opaque data power creates backlash, regulation, and reputational risk.

Open banking is the chance to keep the access benefit while correcting the control failure.

For the Philippines, the opportunity is concrete. A real-time credit exchange like LenderLink can become part of the market's missing risk infrastructure, especially for digital lenders serving thin-file and underbanked borrowers. But the prize is not the API. The prize is a trusted credit market where a borrower can carry a real financial reputation from one provider to the next.

That is what open banking has always promised.

The old bureau model made credit information portable for lenders. The next model has to make financial reputation portable for customers.
